Most beginner explanations flatten "the mark of self, the mark of person, the mark of being, and the mark of a life-span" into a single idea — "don't have a fixed identity." That's not wrong, but it skips the structure that makes this passage do real work.

## Reading the sequence, not just the list

- **Mark of self (我相)** — the raw sense of "I."
- **Mark of person (人相)** — "I" defined against "others," a social self.
- **Mark of being (衆生相)** — "I" as one instance of a category, a member of a class of beings.
- **Mark of a life-span (壽者相)** — "I" as a continuous thread across time, birth to death.

Each mark is a further layer of construction laid on top of the last. The sutra isn't naming four unrelated errors — it's tracing how a bare moment of experience gets built, step by step, into a permanent, separate, storied self.

## Why the order matters for practice

If you only attack the first mark ("I am not a fixed self") without seeing how the later marks reconstruct it — through social comparison, category identity, and narrative continuity — you'll dissolve the sense of self in meditation and rebuild it by lunchtime through the very mechanisms the sutra names.

## A harder question for advanced students

Which of the four marks is doing the most construction work in your current sense of self this year — self, person, being, or life-span? Sit with that specifically, not with "self" in the abstract.
